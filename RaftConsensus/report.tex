\section{Implementation of the Election Algorithm}

\subsection{System Initialization and Heartbeats}
In the Raft consensus algorithm, servers operate in one of the following states \cite{184040,tanenbaum2007distributed}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follower
    \item Candidate
    \item Leader
\end{itemize}
All servers are initialized as Followers \cite{184040,tanenbaum2007distributed}.
A Leader maintains its authority by broadcasting periodic heartbeat messages,
through empty \texttt{AppendEntries} RPCs \cite{184040,tanenbaum2007distributed}.

\subsection{Initiating an Election}
If a Follower does not receive a heartbeat within a specific timeframe,
known as the \textit{"election timeout"}, it assumes the Leader has failed \cite{184040, tanenbaum2007distributed}.
At this point, the Follower transitions to the Candidate state,
increments its current \texttt{term number}, votes for itself, and broadcasts
\texttt{RequestVote} RPCs to the other servers in the cluster \cite{184040,tanenbaum2007distributed}.

\subsection{Processing Vote Requests}
Normally, a \texttt{RequestVote} RPC contains the candidate's \texttt{lastLogIndex} 
and \texttt{lastLogTerm} to allow voters to determine which candidate's log is 
more up-to-date \cite{184040}.
However, the assignment description specifies that \textit{"all AppendEntries RPC
carry empty log messages and no node ever appends anything to its log"}.
In this particular case, the evaluation of the \texttt{RequestVote} RPC will be
based only on the \texttt{term number}, rejecting the request if the candidate's
term is lower than its own, and giving the vote if it has not already voted in
the current term \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.

\section{Election Mechanics with Empty Logs}
So in this case the leader election follows a first-come-first-served logic,
based on \texttt{term numbers}.
The Raft protocol dictates that a candidate may only be elected if its log
is at least as up-to-date as any other log in that majority \cite{184040}.
In our example, the logs are empty and thus, identical, so this condition is
automatically respected.

\subsection{Securing Leadership}
Thus, a follower will grant its vote to the first candidate that requests it
during a new term \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.
Upon securing votes from a majority of the servers, including its own vote, a
candidate promotes itself to the leader state.
It then immediately dispatches heartbeat messages to assert its leadership and
suppress further elections \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.
If a candidate receives a heartbeat from an entity claiming to be leader with a
term number equal to or greater than its own, it acknowledges the leader and reverts
to the follower state \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.

\section{Vulnerability to Election Failures}
\subsection{Split Votes}
Despite the simplified state of the logs, elections can still fail due to split votes \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.
If multiple followers experience an election timeout simultaneously, they will transition
into candidates, increment their term numbers, and cast votes for themselves at the
exact same time \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.
Because the remaining servers allocate their votes on a first-come-first-served basis,
the votes may be distributed in such a way that no single candidate achieves
the requisite majority \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.

\subsection{Mitigation with Randomized Timeouts}
This results in a failed election and a stalemate that persists until a candidate
timeout occurs \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.
At this point, the candidates increment their term numbers and
initiate a subsequent election round \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.
To mitigate the risk of indefinite split-vote loops, Raft employs randomized
election timeouts on a per-server basis \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.
By assigning timeouts randomly from a fixed interval, such as 150 to 300 milliseconds,
the protocol ensures that it is highly probable for one server to time out before
its peers \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.
This early candidate can then secure the majority vote and broadcast
heartbeats to establish its leadership before a split vote can manifest \cite{tanenbaum2007distributed}.
